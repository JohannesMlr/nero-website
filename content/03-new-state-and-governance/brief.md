# New State and Governance Models — brief

## Current state

The working hypothesis of this field is now sharper than "the state is too slow". It is that Europe's
governance problem is not primarily a shortage of good proposals — the Draghi report supplied 170-odd
proposal clusters and the implementation index has barely moved — but an atrophied capacity to set and
pursue goals at all. Diagnosis is abundant; the instrument that converts diagnosis into direction,
mandate and measurable progress is missing.

That reframes the field's four target attributes — speed, efficiency, accountability, trust — as
downstream effects of one upstream failure rather than as four reform projects. The load-bearing claim:
**a society without clear goals and a society suffocating in regulation and bureaucracy are two sides of
the same coin.** Where outcomes are never specified, process compliance is the only available form of
control, and bureaucracy is the rational result. Specify outcomes and control can be traded for autonomy,
local initiative and speed. This makes the case against over-regulation an engineering argument rather
than an ideological one, which is a stronger place to argue from.

Two institutional responses are on file and they operate at different layers. The **Rhine Group**
(Aug 2026) is a convening bet: assemble enough senior credibility around the Draghi agenda that national
and EU decision-making unblocks. It has no formal powers, no budget authority and no treaty standing —
and its own legitimacy is contestable (Swiss-law registration, US-hosted infrastructure, effectively no
representation from post-2004 member states). **Goal-based political leadership** (Nov 2025, own writing)
argues one layer lower: the missing capacity is the ability to translate analysis into goals a public can
recognise — value-oriented rather than input-oriented, explicit about who benefits versus who must
contribute, and sliced into early indicators that let progress be shown and course corrected. The
defence example is the cleanest test case in the file: €100bn Sondervermögen and 5 per cent of GDP are
input goals that function as budget instruments and as promises to allies, and fail entirely as
activation of a population.

The honest gap in our own position: we have a persuasive case for the instrument and no implementation
design for it. Applying goal practice from organisations to democratic politics runs into the problem
that a government's goals need an electoral mandate and a state cannot dismiss those who disagree.
Legitimacy, not technique, is the hard part — and so far we have treated it largely as a communication
problem.

## Cross-referenced finds

- **Rhine Group** (Aug 2026) — Draghi/Collison reform forum. Relevant here as a governance experiment: a body with no formal powers betting that convening power plus commissioned evidence can unblock a stalled reform agenda. Baseline to judge it against: 60 of 383 Draghi recommendations fully implemented by July 2026 (15.7%). It also raises the governance question in its purest form: who authorises reform when the elected machinery cannot deliver its own best analysis? The group's Swiss registration, US-hosted infrastructure and near-absent representation from post-2004 member states make that question harder, not easier, to answer in its favour. ## Open questions / what to watch next

- **Where is the legitimacy design?** Public goal-setting needs a mandate mechanism — manifesto commitments, coalition agreements, statutory targets, citizen assemblies? Until we can name one, the outcome-over-process argument stays a management argument wearing political clothes.
- Part 2 of the goals essay (goal *processes* and routines in parties, governments, administration) is announced but unwritten. That is where the machinery would have to be specified — worth prioritising, since it is the field's most obvious own contribution.
- Which European government or agency is **already** running outcome-based steering with published early indicators? We need at least one working European example; without it the field argues from corporate practice and foreign models only.
- Does the Rhine Group produce named, costed, politically sponsored reform packages — the test of whether convening power alone unblocks anything?
- The uncomfortable evidence problem: the most communicatively effective goal-setting on offer comes from Chinese five-year plans and US campaign slogans. What does a distinctly European, democratically legitimate version of ambitious goal-setting look like — and can we describe it concretely rather than by negation?
- Statutory targets have a bad European track record (climate targets missed without consequence). Under what conditions does a public goal create accountability rather than a ritual of non-compliance?
