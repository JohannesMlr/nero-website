## Summary

**This is NERO's own writing** — an essay by Johannes Müller on his Substack *Renaissance Thoughts*
(23 November 2025), published bilingually with the German text first and the author's own English
version below it. Treat it as a foundation text and internal position, not as third-party evidence:
it can be built on and quoted from, but it cannot be cited as independent corroboration of its own
claims.

The argument: Western societies, and Germany and Europe in particular, have stopped setting shared
goals. Political communication has collapsed into the defensive — "Renten sichern, Frieden sichern,
Wohlstand sichern" — and politicians, parties and administrations have become administrators of the
status quo rather than leaders of it. Meanwhile China runs expansive five-year plans and large parts
of the US rally behind "make America great again"; Europe often seems unsure whether it has joint
goals at all. The essay names the deeper cause honestly: goal-avoidance is not only conflict-avoidance
but a symptom of a pluralistic, individualistic society with no answer left to "who are we, together,
and what do we want to become?" — a question the author explicitly defers to a companion text.

Part 1 (this piece) sets out three quality criteria for effective goals, drawn from the author's near
decade of work building goal-based operating models in organisations of up to hundreds of thousands
of people. **Value orientation:** distinguish input / output / outcome / impact goals along an impact
chain, and note that the dimension most relevant to people — personal value — is the one most often
skipped; getting there takes three to five iterations of "why". **Audience awareness:** separate who
*benefits* from a goal from who must *contribute* to it, and speak in the language of the latter.
**Fast measurability:** slice goals into time periods with tangible early indicators, so progress can
be shown, small wins celebrated, setbacks explained, and the actors who can move the number held
publicly to account. It closes on focus: Adenauer wanted Western integration, Brandt rapprochement
with the East, Kohl reunification and European integration — chancellors remembered as effective are
the ones who subordinated their apparatus to one BHAG. Part 2, on goal *processes* and routines in
parties, governments and administrations, is announced but not yet filed here.

## Why it matters

This is the field's first primary source, and it supplies what Field 03 otherwise lacks: a mechanism.
The field's guiding question asks how to rebuild the state as a system defined by speed, efficiency,
accountability and trust. The essay's sharpest claim is that these are not four separate reform
projects but downstream effects of one missing instrument — **a society without clear goals and a
society suffocating in regulation and bureaucracy are two sides of the same coin.** If outcomes are
never specified, the only available form of control is process compliance; specify outcomes and you
can trade control for autonomy, local initiative and speed. That reframes deregulation from a
political preference into an engineering consequence, and it is a more defensible position than the
usual "cut red tape" register.

It also connects directly to the Rhine Group find already on file. That group exists because the
Draghi diagnosis did not move the machine, and it is betting that convening power plus commissioned
evidence will. This essay suggests the missing link sits one layer lower: not more analysis and not
more convening, but the capacity to translate analysis into goals that a public can recognise, that
name who contributes, and that show early progress. Read together, the two finds frame Field 03's
central tension — Europe's reform problem may be less a shortage of good proposals than an atrophied
governing muscle for setting and pursuing goals at all. The defence example in the essay is the
cleanest illustration: €100bn Sondervermögen and 5 per cent of GDP are *input* goals that work as
budget instruments and as promises to allies, and fail completely as activation.

**Caveats to keep honest.** First, the essay is a transfer argument — corporate OKR practice applied
to democratic politics — and it does not fully confront where the analogy breaks: a company's goals
need no electoral mandate, and a firm can fire the people who disagree. Legitimacy, not technique, is
the hard part of public goal-setting, and the piece treats it mostly as a communication problem.
Second, its own most striking evidence for outcome-orientation ("Build the Wall" as communicatively
effective, expansive Chinese five-year plans as a benchmark) points at regimes and campaigns NERO
would not otherwise hold up — the essay is aware of this and says so, but the tension is real and
worth naming rather than smoothing over. Third, Part 2 is where the actual governance machinery would
have to be specified; until it exists, this is a persuasive case for an instrument without an
implementation design.

## Quotable

- "Save the pensions. Keep peace. Protect prosperity." — the author's rendering of the defensive register of contemporary political communication (German original: „Renten sichern, Frieden sichern, Wohlstand sichern.")
- "A society without clear goals and a society suffocated by regulation and bureaucracy are two sides of the same coin."
- "When outcomes matter more than compliance with process, systems can remain more adaptive, while performing better and faster."
- "Every social conflict is, at its core, a conflict of goals."
- "China successfully pursues expansive five-year plans, with great confidence. Large parts of the United States, no less assertively, rally behind the ambition to 'make America great again.' Europe, by contrast, often seems unsure whether it has joint goals at all."
- "Who are we, together? And what do we, collectively, want to become?"
- **60–70%** of employees in organisations at the start of an engagement say they do not know their organisation's goals or how they contribute to them.
- Aladin El-Mafaalani, *Misstrauensgemeinschaften*: societies with a positive outlook on the future can endure hardship and long periods of strain; the downward spiral of mutual suspicion and radicalisation begins only when trust in a better tomorrow erodes.
