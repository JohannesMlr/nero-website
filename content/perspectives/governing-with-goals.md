_**TL;DR:** As a society, we lack clear, unifying goals right now — goals that could guide us out of a current state of stagnation, that could motivate and activate. Shared goals are essential for democratic discourse, to keep bureaucracy in check, drive progress, and to revive a sense of collective confidence. While this text focuses on practical tools for political communication, another essay deals with the deeper, closely connected challenge — the absence of a clear collective identity, on a local as well as on the European level._

"Save the pensions. Keep peace. Protect prosperity." Political communication today has become defensive on all levels. It focuses on preserving what we have, not on improving or moving forward. We are witnessing the quiet disappearance of political and societal goals — the very kind that are required for progress and momentum.

That may be because, in uncertain times, most people simply wish for things to stay as they are. Perhaps leaders fear overwhelming citizens with ambition, sensing that there is no demand for new goals. Maybe it is because ambitious goals make you politically vulnerable — easier to attack.

It may also be that the sheer speed of change, together with the constant noise of our media ecosystem, has forced political actors into a constant, defensive and reactive mode — one that makes genuine long-term vision increasingly difficult.

On a deeper level, though, the avoidance of clear goals is not only a tactic for managing risk or avoiding conflict. It is also a symptom of a pluralistic and individualistic society that no longer has a clear answer to two simple but defining questions:

Who are we, together? And what do we, collectively, want to become? Those questions, however, deserve a text of their own. This one is about what happens when we stop asking them — and how we might start again.

## Our Collective Aimlessness is a Problem

Even to simply preserve what we have, we must now move and develop at high speed. Our world order has been changing dramatically for years — and continues to do so. China successfully pursues expansive five-year plans, with great confidence. Large parts of the United States, no less assertively, rally behind the ambition to "make America great again." Europe, by contrast, often seems unsure whether it has joint goals at all.

Politicians, parties, and public institutions have increasingly turned into administrators — managers of the status quo. Many seem to have forgotten how to lead, how to work effectively with serious, activating, and ambitious goals. In Germany, you could say that the power-preservation strategy of the Merkel-Scholz years has left its mark. But defining and negotiating goals is not just a question of governance or management. It can also be a process of identity-building and activation — one that includes and informs citizens, that makes democracy tangible and alive. Without that process, democracy becomes quiet and mechanical.

Every social conflict is, at its core, a conflict of goals. No society — and no political sphere — can really exist without those. The problem is that much of today's political leadership has given away the discussion of these conflicting goals to other, oftentimes more radical groups. As a result, conflicts get amplified in echo chambers without resolution, instead of being addressed productively.

There is another dramatic consequence: A society without clear goals and a society suffocated by regulation and bureaucracy are two sides of the same coin. Systematically working with goals provides the foundation for autonomy, for local initiative and speed. Clear goals and aspired outcomes allow for more freedom in choosing the ways to achieve them — and less need for control. When outcomes matter more than compliance with process, systems can remain more adaptive, while performing better and faster.

Last but not least: a lack of shared goals stifles the momentum and confidence a society needs. Kennedy's "Let's put a man on the moon" is still considered a case study on how a single, ambitious goal can mobilize an entire nation — unleashing unimaginable resources and innovation. Common goals can activate collective effort, unlock energy, and create a sense of meaning — as described by thinkers like Viktor Frankl, Daniel Pink, and many others. Goals attract and motivate talent; they provide orientation for interdisciplinary collaboration and a blueprint for how organizations function. Working with clear objectives can strengthen a culture of experimentation and perseverance. In that sense, goals could help our society develop an essential — and currently undertrained — muscle. In his book _Misstrauensgemeinschaften_, Aladin El-Mafaalani describes how societies with a positive outlook on the future can endure hardship and withstand long periods of strain. Only when trust in a better tomorrow erodes, downward spirals of mutual suspicion and radicalization begin.

## My Own Experiences: What Goals Can Do

For almost ten years, I've been working with large organizations — regularly with hundreds of thousands of people — enabling them to execute better and become more adaptive, by building their operating models around outcomes and goals instead of processes and hierarchies.

Through that work, I have witnessed the transformative energy of successful goal processes, the force of people who know how to really lead with goals (usually more important than the goals themselves), but also the many challenges that come with it.

What fascinates me most: Working effectively with goals touches and tests almost every element of a system — whether it's an organization, a society, or each of us as individuals. Goals don't just express the wishes of those who set them.

They also always open deeper questions: What do we value? Who are we — and who do we want to become? What resources and abilities do we have — and what must we learn or develop to reach the goal? A well-defined goal connects the reality of the present with the vision of a desired future. It demands an honest analysis of where we stand, a proper evaluation of our resources, and an understanding of how we allocate them.

Observing companies or communities pursuing a set of goals reveals much about their ability to learn and to reflect — about their adaptability, discipline, and endurance. Leading and coordinating successfully with goals requires the continuous development of the organization that pursues them. In that sense, goals are not only tools for communication, planning, and execution. They are also instruments for ongoing reflection and organizational learning.

One last point: Even the best-run goal processes depend on capable, charismatic people — individuals whose personality injects trust, persistence, and energy into the joint pursuit of a goal. Without such leadership, even the most advanced goal systems remain lifeless.

## Content, Process, Context: The Three Core Components of Effective Goal Work

Whether in companies or in public administration — effective goal work always relies on the interplay of communication and organization, of people and systems.

The central question is the same everywhere: How can a goal become a practical tool for better work, better coordination, and better results?

In this text I focus on goals as political communication and leadership tools. For that purpose, some of the following examples simplify the matter. They are meant to illustrate key ideas rather than cover the full complexity of the topics at hand.

## Part 1 — The Content of a Goal

In most large organizations, entire departments are devoted to the communication of results to shareholders and the media — while almost no one spends enough time to effectively communicate a company's goals to the employees who are supposed to produce those results.

When we begin our work with an organization, typically between 60 and 70 percent of the employees say they don't know what the organization's goals are, nor how their work contributes to achieving them. Experience and data have shown, however, that the quality of goals — in wording, in clarity and precision — is an important cornerstone for achieving them.

When defining the quality of a goal, some elements are critical. The most important among them is a focus on value.

### What is the value we want to create with this goal?

Whether it's a New Year's resolution or a corporate strategy, people interpret the idea of a "goal" very differently. Too often, goals already fail as communication and coordination tools because they are defined carelessly. Nobody sees relevance or meaning in them and hence, there is not even a serious attempt to pursue the goal.

That is why it makes sense, especially for political communication, to distinguish between four types of goals — four dimensions that are closely connected in a chain of impact:

- **Input goals:** How we invest resources.
- **Output goals:** The tangible products or deliverables of our work or investments.
- **Outcome goals:** The results or effects of those outputs — ideally with a measurable short term value.
- **Impact goals:** The deeper, rather long-term effects — usually financial, social, or personal value created.

Successful change requires the alignment of goals and decisions across all of these four levels — from input to impact.

In political or public communication, simplification is necessary: usually only one dimension can be conveyed effectively at a time. Ironically, the most relevant one — personal value (outcomes) — is oftentimes neglected the most.

Our brains tend to frame goals in terms of tangible activities or projects — what we call "outputs." That's why such goals work so well in campaigns: they are visual, symbolic, and easy to remember. You don't have to agree with the project or policy to recognize that Donald Trump's "Build the Wall" was, in its blunt simplicity, a highly effective example of goal communication for a specific audience.

However, for deep, long-term change and for building durable democratic majorities, most political goals today lack clarity and value orientation — mostly on the "outcome" and "impact" levels. Ultimately, every conversation about value leads back to human needs and emotions — or to very personal measures of time and money.

So, if you want to get closer to these more value oriented goals, it is worth asking (in most team situations, multiple times) why a particular goal matters. As Simon Sinek famously puts it: "Start with Why." That way, a well-phrased goal reveals the deeper meaning of an initiative — and activates people emotionally.

Nonetheless, this is also where political communication can become thin or generic. No matter the personal beliefs or political preferences, almost everyone wants prosperity, security, a good life for their children, and a positive outlook on the future. In a different time and political context, Barack Obama for example could successfully build his platform and messaging on these universal values. Today, however, such language risks sounding detached from people's real struggles — or too vague to compete in the crowded marketplace of more aggressive political promises.

In times like these, politicians cannot rely solely on overused abstractions like "growth" or "security." They must translate those values into more tangible experiences that face the current realities and are embedded in the current context.

Take European defense budgets as an example to discuss the value of a goal: A prominent goal in German politics, announced by chancellor Scholz, was to invest an extra €100 billion in special funds ("Doppelwumms") into defense. Later, the goal was defined as investing 5 percent of the German GDP in defense.

As planning tools, or as commitments to allies, these are valid input goals. But as the main communication to voters, particularly in a country that has a rather distanced attitude towards its own military, they fail to convince or to activate.

An output discussion is necessary as well — for instance, how exactly those funds are used, or whether tanks are still the right investment after the experiences from the war in Ukraine. But again, for most non-expert citizens, that debate feels rather technical and distant.

Even years after Russia's invasion, German politicians struggle to translate the value of these investments for the population — into simple, emotionally resonant goals with a compelling narrative that people can hold onto.

What would be the personal costs of an open war on NATO soil — for every citizen? How can we express "security" and "sovereignty" not as abstract policies but as everyday life experiences?

### Customer Orientation and Audience Awareness — Who Benefits and Who Contributes?

In the corporate world, leaders regularly fail to drive action through goals because they communicate them in the wrong direction. Their communication focuses entirely on high level financial metrics — numbers that matter to shareholders but less so to employees or customers.

These figures define success at the top, but the actual path to achieving them always runs through the people who directly work with the customer and create value there. That is where activation, coordination, and commitment are required the most.

The same principle applies in politics. We have to ask: Who exactly is meant to benefit from a goal? And who is asked to change or to contribute in order to achieve it?

Political goals should distinguish between the groups that stand to gain — for example parents, retirees, small business owners — and those whose effort or sacrifice is required — like civil servants, taxpayers, or heirs. Without that clarity, relevant goals lose their grounding in the real lives of people.

Take climate policy as an example: Achieving the goal of limiting global warming to 1.5 degrees would, in theory, benefit everyone. But its achievement depends largely on major emitters in industry and transport. Political communication around this goal has never really explained the personal benefits of achieving it — a challenge, no doubt — but even less so has it reached those most responsible for contributing.

Where is the vision for how we could use our market position to lead in clean-energy technologies, as China does by now? Where is the step-by-step story of how Europe can, through carbon tariffs on imports, keep competition fair and protect its workers? If politics is to succeed on that front, it must learn to present climate issues not as moral imperatives but as economic opportunities.

The years under the "Ampel (traffic-light)" coalition in Germany have shown what can happen when the public feels like the wrong stakeholders benefit — or that no one benefits at all. Well-intended, relevant goals ended up dividing, doing a disservice to the cause.

To build real alignment, leaders must speak the language of those they need to convince. That means seeing issues from their perspective, understanding their motivations, and addressing their doubts directly. Parties that are more diverse in both people and experience have a natural advantage here. They can communicate credibly and authentically to different groups and milieus because, having those voices within their own team.

During the 2024/25 Bundestag campaign, I had the chance to observe a candidate and his party up close. His challenge was clear: in order to win, he needed to attract swing voters and supporters of other parties. Yet his goals and his communication were almost entirely aimed at his core base. There seemed to be little attention for the priorities of those who still needed to be persuaded. He did not win his seat.

### Early Measurability — Showing Progress and Adjusting Course Quickly

Many Germans still remember a failed promise of the Merz government: that by the summer of 2025 — just months after the election — people would feel a noticeable improvement of the overall mood in the country.

Political change almost always takes longer than we hope. Governments regularly reap the benefits of their predecessors' good policies, and they lose elections because the positive effects of their own decisions come too late — or require sacrifice before reward.

That is why it is so important to break down big goals into time-bound stages, to identify early, tangible indicators of progress, and to communicate them clearly.

Particularly in times of low trust and high pressure, people need visible signs that things are going in the right direction — measurable milestones that allow people to strengthen their belief in success along the way. Here again, it helps to return to the different types of goals within the chain of impact — input, output, outcome, and impact.

Take economic growth, for example: this is the ultimate impact goal — driven by countless factors and difficult to influence directly through political action. Especially if it is meant to be sustainable growth, not just temporarily tuned up through government debts.

So instead of communicating goals for long-term growth rates or relying on general sentiment indexes, politicians should publicly establish and emphasize lead indicators — early growth drivers they can actually influence. These might for example include numbers of new business registrations, investment in education and innovation, or regional infrastructure improvements.

Once chosen, these indicators should be communicated regularly and explained repeatedly, so people understand why they matter. Ongoing explanation and contextualization is, unfortunately, a skill too rarely displayed in political communication.

Measurable lead indicators don't just show whether a goal will be achieved. They allow leaders to demonstrate progress along the way — to celebrate small wins and to frame setbacks as part of a longer journey. They help keep the public engaged and informed, and they can make collective learning and course-corrections part of the story, instead of becoming a sign of definite failure.

At best, such measurable sub-goals and early indicators create not only a mental connection to a better, more confident future — but also to the actors who are most capable of influencing progress toward those goals. When communicated consistently and transparently, these actors can be held publicly accountable — and, just as importantly, better activated and motivated.

### Focus and Clarity Through Hierarchies of Goals

Some say only a few German chancellors ever governed with a focus on clear and consistent goals — but those who did were the most successful. Adenauer sought Western integration. Brandt pursued reconciliation with the East. Kohl aimed for reunification and European integration.

Most of what these leaders and their administrations accomplished flowed down from those overarching ambitions. In the language of start-ups, such major long-term goals are called BHAGs: Big Hairy Audacious Goals. Great BHAGs can drive focus, predictability and clarity — internally, to the public (customers or voters), and to partners. This also allows for the development of a coherent agenda, a recognizable political identity, even a directional culture that can outlast one limited term in office.

No matter if we speak about groups or individuals, people can only remember — and rally around — two or three defining attributes. The strongest personal and political brands are therefore built on only a handful of clearly chosen values and goals.

Simplicity wins, always. Studying political biographies and talking with politicians today leaves me with another impression: Public life in politics is so intense and fast-moving that anyone without an overarching, anchored goal quickly slips into a purely reactive mode. Politicians respond to events, manage crises, and chase headlines — becoming ever more hasty and superficial in their ways of working.

Without clear, deeply rooted goals, it becomes way harder to decide what to delegate, how to prioritize, and where to invest time and resources. Also in this context, focus is not a constraint; it enables discernment. That is why I suggest politicians should begin their careers — and certainly any campaign — with a clear north-star goal and a focused set of measurable, actionable, and easy-to-communicate sub-goals.

Ideally, the pursuit of this central goal becomes a consistent thread — a guiding principle running through the campaign, the party, the legislative term, and even a political life. History provides encouraging examples that demonstrate, when leaders have such a compass, they do more than manage the present, they can shape the future.
