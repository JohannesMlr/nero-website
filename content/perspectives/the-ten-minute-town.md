The idea sounds almost too simple to be a policy: design towns so that school, work, healthcare, groceries and green space are all within a short walk or ride. But behind that simplicity is a real rebuttal to decades of planning that optimised for cars and commutes over community and contact.

The towns doing this well didn't start with grand infrastructure spending. They started by removing the small frictions — a closed-off street reopened to foot traffic, a vacant storefront turned into a shared workspace, a school playground that doubles as a public park after hours.

The payoff shows up in metrics planners care about — lower emissions, higher local spending — but also in ones harder to measure: neighbours who know each other's names, and communities with enough daily contact to organise when it matters.
