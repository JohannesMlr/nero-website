"The talent isn't the bottleneck," says one educator working to redesign a technical curriculum from the ground up. "Confidence is. Our best students can tell you exactly why a venture might fail. We spend far less time teaching them to try anyway."

That imbalance, she argues, is structural rather than cultural. Grading rewards the correct critique over the risky attempt; career pipelines reward the safe internship over the shaky first company. Changing the incentive, not the students, is where reform has to start.

Some institutions are already testing the fix — giving credit for ventures that fail honestly, pairing technical programmes directly with founders-in-residence, and treating a failed startup on a CV the way a failed experiment is treated in a physics lab: as data, not disgrace.
