## Summary

In August 2026 Mario Draghi and Stripe CEO Patrick Collison launched the **Rhine Group**, a cross-sector forum co-chaired by the two of them and run operationally by the Spanish economist and former MEP **Luis Garicano** as executive director. Its stated purpose is narrow and unusually concrete: take the 2024 Draghi report on European competitiveness — roughly 170 proposal clusters across energy, industry, defence, digital infrastructure, capital markets and productivity — and push them from analysis into implemented reform. The membership deliberately mixes worlds that normally sit at separate tables: economists (Philippe Aghion, Bengt Holmström, Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, Lucrezia Reichlin, Francesco Giavazzi, Beatrice Weder di Mauro), founders and operators (Tobi Lütke / Shopify, Niklas Zennström / Atomico, Sebastian Siemiatkowski / Klarna, Luca Ferrari / Bending Spoons, Xavier Niel / Iliad, Andrea Pignataro / ION), and former officeholders (Toomas Hendrik Ilves, Bruno Le Maire, Vittorio Colao), alongside Zanny Minton Beddoes of The Economist. Reported working model: commissioned expert research plus at least annual convenings.

The group's diagnosis opens on a single number — only four of the world's fifty largest technology companies are European — and escalates from there. Its argument is not industrial prestige but fiscal capacity and sovereignty: a low-growth Europe cannot fund defence, healthcare, pensions, education and climate investment, so stagnation eventually converts into strategic subordination. The manifesto line doing the rounds: decline is not inevitable, but it is the current direction of travel absent urgent action.

## Why it matters

This is a **confirmation of NERO's core premise from an establishment source** — and worth noting that the establishment is now saying it in NERO's register rather than the usual defensive one. The Rhine Group explicitly rejects protecting what exists in favour of rebuilding Europe's capacity to invent, build and scale, and it frames technology leadership as an input to sovereignty rather than a sector policy. That is close to Field 02's own tagline. The composition is also a signal in itself: the reform coalition being assembled is founder-heavy, not commission-heavy.

The more interesting question for us is the **implementation gap it implicitly concedes**. The group exists because reports did not move the machine. Europe's problem was never a shortage of diagnosis — it was, as Garicano put it, a blocked reform agenda. A forum with no formal powers, no budget authority and no treaty standing is betting that convening power plus commissioned evidence can unblock national and EU-level decision-making. That is a governance hypothesis (Field 03) as much as a technology one, and it is unproven.

**Counter-signal to keep on file:** the European Policy Innovation Council's Draghi Observatory found that as of July 2026, only 60 of 383 tracked recommendations had been fully implemented — 15.7 per cent — with the index moving just 0.6 percentage points over six months. Two years after the report, implementation is close to flat. That is the honest baseline against which the Rhine Group should be judged, and it cuts both ways: it justifies the group's existence and it is evidence that this class of intervention has so far not worked.

A second caution: a body of sixty-plus senior figures with an annual meeting rhythm is structurally closer to a high-status convening than to a delivery vehicle. The test is whether it produces named, costed, politically sponsored reform packages that a specific government or Commissioner picks up — not whether it produces another well-argued document.

**Legitimacy counter-signals (added 29 Aug 2026):** two structural weaknesses will be used against the group and are worth naming before its opponents do. First, **domicile and stack**: a body arguing for European sovereignty is registered under Swiss law with offices at a Geneva trust company, and runs its own website on Webflow (San Francisco) hosted on AWS servers in Northern Virginia with Fastly in front — while a significant share of members hold senior roles at US firms (Mastercard, Morgan Stanley, General Catalyst) and co-chair Collison's Stripe keeps a large San Francisco presence. Second, **representation**: of the 55 members Brussels Signal counted, none are from Poland and only one — former Estonian president Toomas Hendrik Ilves — comes from any post-2004 accession state. A reform coalition that is unelected, Swiss-domiciled and weighted to the pre-2004 West can be dismissed as Davos with a better slide deck long before a single proposal is debated on its merits. A third, avoidable liability: the inclusion of Leopold Aschenbrenner, whose leveraged AI fund lost roughly $35bn in July 2026 and whose trading banks were subsequently subpoenaed by the SEC — with Collison among the fund's early backers.

None of this makes the diagnosis wrong. It does mean the group's own conduct is now part of its argument: an organisation claiming Europe can build must be visibly willing to build on Europe.
